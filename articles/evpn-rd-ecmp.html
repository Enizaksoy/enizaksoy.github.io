One RD, Two Leaves

RFC 7432 §7.9 requires the RD to be unique within a PE and recommends a loopback-derived Type-1 value. Whether it must also differ between PEs is not in the RFC — it is in vendor guidance, and in what the RIB actually does. Measured on two EVPN implementations, in four topologies, with the raw output.

1. The claim

An EVPN design argument that recurs often enough to be worth settling with a lab rather than with opinion:

The claim. "With a route reflector — yes, you need a unique RD. With direct peering you don't. At least on Cisco; they even publish examples using the same RD."

It sounds reasonable, and it is partly true. It is also cheap to test: put the same prefix behind two leaves, receive it on a third, and change exactly one variable.

The short version of what follows: a shared RD can produce ECMP, but only in a topology nobody builds, and only after enabling a control that appears in no standard fabric template. The moment anything re-advertises the route — a route reflector, a transit spine — the second path stops existing at all, and no amount of multipath configuration brings it back.

2. What the RFC actually says

"The RFC requires unique RDs" is repeated a lot. It is worth reading the actual text, because it does not say what people think it says. RFC 7432 §7.9, Route Distinguisher Assignment per MAC-VRF:

"The Route Distinguisher (RD) MUST be set to the RD of the MAC-VRF that is advertising the NLRI. An RD MUST be assigned for a given MAC-VRF on a PE. This RD MUST be unique across all MAC-VRFs on a PE. It is RECOMMENDED to use the Type 1 RD [RFC4364]. The value field comprises an IP address of the PE (typically, the loopback address) followed by a number unique to the PE."

Read it carefully:

  • The normative MUST is about uniqueness within a single PE — don't reuse one RD across two MAC-VRFs on the same box.
  • Uniqueness between PEs is never stated as a requirement. It emerges as a side effect of the RECOMMENDED Type-1 format, whose administrator field is the PE's own loopback. Cisco's rd auto and its equivalents implement exactly that recommendation, which is why unique-per-PE RDs are what everyone actually runs.
  • §7.9 is about the MAC-VRF — Layer 2. The IP Prefix route (Type-5) that carries a tenant subnet across an L3VNI is not in RFC 7432 at all; it is defined in RFC 9136. So for the symmetric-IRB case tested here, §7.9 is context, not authority.
So the RFC does not settle it. Anyone arguing "the RFC mandates a unique RD per PE" is overstating, and anyone arguing "the RFC permits a shared RD, therefore it is fine" is answering a standards question when the real question is a forwarding question. The gap is exactly what a lab is for.

3. RD is not RT, and the two get conflated constantly

Both are written ASN:nn. They do entirely different jobs, and the discussion turns to noise when they are mixed up.

Route DistinguisherRoute Target
What it isPart of the NLRI — inside the route's identityAn extended community — a label attached to the route
JobMake two PEs' copies of one prefix distinct BGP objectsDecide which VRF imports the route
Should beUnique per PEShared across the fabric
Where it appearsThe Route Distinguisher: section headerThe Extcommunity: RT:… line under each path

Making the RT unique per PE does not tighten anything — it stops the import and the tenant route never reaches the VRF. So "unique RD and RT" is never the right phrase. It is unique RD, shared RT, and every capture below keeps the RT fixed at 65000:5000 while only the RD moves.

ubest/mbest: 2/0 is the number to watch for the rest of this article. tag is the origin AS and tunnelid is the VTEP loopback in hex — 0x1010101 = 1.1.1.1, 0x2020202 = 2.2.2.2 — so it is visible at a glance that the two paths really are two different VTEPs.

6. Shared RD behind a transit node — it cannot work, and not for the reason people assume

Now set rd 65000:5000 in vrf context Customer-A on both origins, change nothing else, and the receiver drops to one path:

Leaf-3# show ip route 172.16.80.0/24 vrf Customer-A
172.16.80.0/24, ubest/mbest: 1/0
    *via 1.1.1.1%default, [20/0], 00:00:53, bgp-65003, external, tag 65001, segid: 50000 tunnelid: 0x1010101 encap: VXLAN

The intuitive explanation is that best-path discarded the other leaf. Look at what actually arrived instead:

Leaf-3# show bgp l2vpn evpn 172.16.80.0
Route Distinguisher: 65000:5000
BGP routing table entry for [5]:[0]:[0]:[24]:[172.16.80.0]/224, version 365
Paths: (2 available, best #2)

  Path type: external, path is valid, not best reason: Router Id, no labeled nexthop
  AS-Path: 65000 65002 , path sourced external to AS
    2.2.2.2 (metric 0) from 10.1.23.2 (100.1.1.2)
      Extcommunity: RT:65000:5000 ENCAP:8 Router MAC:529a.5b2c.1b08

  Path type: external, path is valid, is best path, no labeled nexthop
             Imported to 2 destination(s)
  AS-Path: 65000 65002 , path sourced external to AS
    2.2.2.2 (metric 0) from 10.1.13.2 (100.1.1.1)
      Extcommunity: RT:65000:5000 ENCAP:8 Router MAC:529a.5b2c.1b08
Both paths have next-hop 2.2.2.2 and the same Router MAC. They are not two leaves — they are the same leaf's route arriving through two different spines (from 10.1.13.2 and from 10.1.23.2). The other origin never reached the receiver at all.

With a shared RD the two origins produce one NLRI. Each spine runs best-path on that single NLRI and re-advertises exactly one copy. There is no second VTEP anywhere in the receiver's table, so multipath has nothing to select. This is not a tuning problem — the alternative was deleted one hop upstream.

A route reflector behaves identically. The mechanism is not "RR"; it is any node that re-advertises. In a spine-leaf fabric that describes every path a route can take.

The damage is also not limited to one prefix. Comparing the receiver's VRF BGP table across the two runs, the other tenant subnets went from four candidates to three — one PE's entire contribution vanishes fabric-wide:

unique RD                              shared RD
*>r172.16.10.0/24   0.0.0.0   (local)  *>r172.16.10.0/24   0.0.0.0   (local)
*  e               4.4.4.4             *  e               1.1.1.1
*  e               2.2.2.2             *  e               4.4.4.4
*  e               1.1.1.1

7. Shared RD with direct peering — the claim's actual scenario

Testing the claim properly means taking the transit node out of the decision. Direct multihop eBGP sessions were built from the receiver to each origin over loopbacks, leaving the spine sessions in place:

router bgp 65003
  neighbor 1.1.1.1 remote-as 65001
    update-source loopback0
    ebgp-multihop 5
    address-family l2vpn evpn
      send-community
      send-community extended
  neighbor 2.2.2.2 remote-as 65002
    update-source loopback0
    ebgp-multihop 5
    address-family l2vpn evpn
      send-community
      send-community extended
A full mesh is the wrong shape for this test. The first attempt also peered the two origins with each other. Sharing an RD, each one then collapsed its own route and its neighbour's into a single NLRI, chose one, and re-advertised that with its own next-hop and Router MAC. The receiver ended up with AS-paths 65001 65002 and 65002 65001 — length 2, neither being an origin's own route — plus two spine copies that had gone invalid(rnh not resolved) with link-address next-hops. Removing the origin-to-origin session is what isolates the variable.

With receiver-to-origin sessions only, each origin advertises its own route. Verified at the source — the local route wins on weight and is the one sent to the receiver (3.3.3.3):

Note the discontinuity: the EVPN table holds two paths, the VRF table holds one. Something between those two tables dropped a candidate, and it is not maximum-paths — that control never saw a second candidate at all. The clue is one line up:

             Imported to 2 destination(s)     <-- present only under the best path
Reading the tie-breaker. not best reason: Router Id means the comparison ran all the way to the router-ID and 1.1.1.1 < 2.2.2.2 decided it. Nothing is wrong with the losing path — it lost an arbitrary tie-break. In the VRF table the same loss is reported as Neighbor Address, because bestpath compare-neighborid is configured there.

Two textual changes explain the whole mechanism. The losing path gained the word multipath, and Imported to 2 destination(s) now appears under both paths. VRF import follows the best path plus multipath members — so until a path becomes a multipath member it is never imported, and the VRF-level maximum-paths never gets a chance to consider it.

So the claim is vindicated in its narrow form: shared RD with direct peering does give ECMP. It needs a control absent from the default template, and that control is unnecessary in any design using a unique RD.

9. Two multipath layers, and which one you actually need

ControlQuestion it answersNeeded when
address-family l2vpn evpn
  maximum-paths N		How many paths of the same EVPN NLRI become multipath members — and therefore get imported into the VRF?Only when PEs share an RD
vrf X / address-family ipv4 unicast
  maximum-paths N		How many of the VRF's candidates get installed into the routing table?Always

With unique RDs the first control is redundant: each PE's route is already its own NLRI with its own best path, so two candidates reach the VRF without any EVPN-level multipath at all. That is why the standard template omits it — and why nobody discovers it exists until they break the RD.

10. A different RIB model, and why the answer does not port

The same test was run on a second EVPN implementation — Versa VOS, symmetric IRB, L3 VNI 110, spine route reflectors, test prefix 10.1.80.0/24 originated by two leaves. The routing-table outcome matches, but the mechanism does not, which matters if you are tempted to carry the Cisco answer across.

With unique RDs the receiver shows something an NX-OS engineer does not expect:

One entry. Reason: identifier is the router-ID tie-break — the direct analogue of not best reason: Router Id on NX-OS. Exactly the topology that produced two paths on NX-OS once the EVPN-level knob was added produces one here.

This is a model difference, not a missing feature. Cisco exposes multipath as a separate control at the EVPN NLRI layer, which is what allows a shared-RD topology to be rescued. VOS reaches ECMP a different way — by keeping per-RD entries as distinct RIB objects — so in the supported design it needs no control at all, and the only place such a control would help is a configuration both vendors advise against. Its path-selection tuning is deliberately narrow: 
path-selection ?
  always-compare-med       Always compare MED values, regardless of neighbor AS
  as-path-ignore           Exclude AS-path from BGP best path computation
  as-path-multipath-relax  Allow differing AS paths to be considered for multipath if their lengths are equal
  cisco-non-deterministic  Use Cisco IOS nondeterministic path selection algorithm
as-path-multipath-relax governs which paths are eligible, not how many are installed.

The practical consequence: an argument that holds on one platform because of a specific control cannot be assumed to hold on another that reaches the same outcome by a different route. Where the RIB keys on the RD, the RD is not a stylistic choice — it is the mechanism.

11. Vendor guidance

The claim included "they even publish examples with the same RD". The configuration guide is not neutral. Cisco Nexus 9000 Series NX-OS VXLAN Configuration Guide, Release 10.5(x), Configure VXLAN BGP EVPN → Supported features and configuration limits → Configuration recommendations:

"In a VXLAN EVPN setup, border nodes must be configured with unique route distinguishers, preferably using the auto rd command. Not using unique route distinguishers across all border nodes is not supported. The use of unique route distinguishers is strongly recommended for all VTEPs of a fabric."

Two different strengths in one bullet, worth keeping straight: not supported for border nodes, strongly recommended for VTEPs generally. Combined with §2, the hierarchy is: the RFC requires per-PE-internal uniqueness and recommends a format that produces per-PE uniqueness; the vendor turns that recommendation into a support boundary; the lab shows why.

12. Measurement caveats

Three things that nearly produced wrong conclusions, recorded because they are easy traps to fall into again.

  • The forwarding-table command is misleading on a virtual platform. show forwarding … route … detail reported No of paths: 1 even for a plain underlay loopback the routing table showed as ubest/mbest: 2/0: 
    Leaf-3# show ip route 1.1.1.1/32
1.1.1.1/32, ubest/mbest: 2/0
    *via 10.1.13.2, [20/0], 01:33:13, bgp-65003, external, tag 65000
    *via 10.1.23.2, [20/0], 01:33:14, bgp-65003, external, tag 65000

Leaf-3# show forwarding route 1.1.1.1/32 detail
Prefix 1.1.1.1/32, No of paths: 1, Update time: ...
   10.1.13.2                                 Ethernet1/1
    A control test on a prefix unrelated to VXLAN confirms this is a software-forwarding display characteristic, not evidence about hardware ECMP. Every conclusion here rests on the routing table.
  • Stale state after a session teardown. Removing the origin-to-origin session made the prefix vanish from the receiver entirely — zero paths — while both origins still reported Path-id 1 advertised to peers: 3.3.3.3. A route refresh brought it back with two paths. Taken as the result, that capture would have produced a confident and completely wrong finding.
  • An isolation method can break what you are measuring. On the second platform, shutting the reflector sessions to isolate direct peering also removed what the receiver needed to resolve the VXLAN tunnel, so the forwarding table went empty while the RIB still showed the path as via Indirect(). The BGP-level result stands; the forwarding evidence had to come from the run where the reflectors were still up.

13. Takeaways

  1. Unique RD, shared RT. A sentence proposing to make both unique is proposing to break route import.
  2. The RFC is not the authority people cite it as. §7.9 mandates uniqueness within a PE and recommends a loopback-derived Type-1 RD. Per-PE uniqueness is a consequence of following that recommendation, not a separate MUST — and Type-5 routes live in RFC 9136 anyway.
  3. A shared RD does not "reduce ECMP" — upstream it deletes the alternative. Behind any re-advertising node the receiver's two paths turn out to be one VTEP reached two ways. Check the next-hop, not the path count.
  4. Shared-RD ECMP is achievable on NX-OS with direct PE-to-PE peering plus maximum-paths under the EVPN address family. Direct peering means a full mesh of EVPN sessions — the thing route reflectors exist to avoid.
  5. Do not port the answer between implementations. The same topology gives two paths on one platform and one on another, because the RIB models differ.
  6. There is a second argument that has nothing to do with ECMP. With a shared RD the EVPN table no longer shows which PE originated a route; you fall back to the Router MAC community, the next-hop or the AS-path. rd auto is the default and costs nothing.
The one-line summary. A unique RD is not what turns ECMP on — it is what keeps the candidates alive long enough for multipath to have something to choose from.
